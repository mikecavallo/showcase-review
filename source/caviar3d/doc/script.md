# Great Atlantic: From the Docks to the Tin

A narrated documentary about Great Atlantic Trading Inc. and Caviar Star.
Narration: 2,121 words, about 15.7 minutes at 135 spoken words per minute; about 16.5 to 17 minutes with title
cards and pauses.

Rules for this script
- NARRATION uses only facts in `doc/dossier.md`. SOURCE cites the dossier: V = verified claim, S = single-source
  claim, Q = quote number, T = timeline row (by date), P = products table (section 5).
- Company claims that cannot be checked are spoken as the company's words ("the company says").
- Photos named by file in `doc/research/img/`. 3D renders named by product name and caviarstar.com handle.
- On-screen text never uses em or en dashes. Numbers on screen are the real numbers from the sources.
- Do not label anyone in a photo by name unless `sources.csv` says the company captioned them.

Running time markers are estimates from the word count.

## Chapter 1. Maine and the sea (1:00 to 2:30)

### Scene 1.1
NARRATION:
Portland, Maine. The late nineteen eighties. Off this coast, on rocky bottoms just below the kelp, lives a small spiny animal: the green sea urchin. For years, almost nobody in Maine wanted it. Before 1985, the whole state harvested only about forty five metric tons a year, mostly for small markets in Boston and New York.

VISUAL: Archival-style map, Gulf of Maine, Portland marked. Title card: "Portland, Maine · late 1980s".
Simple illustrated urchin (no stock footage). On screen: "Before 1985: about 45 metric tons a year".

SOURCE: T "Before 1985" (Maine DMR); T "Late 1980s".

### Scene 1.2
NARRATION:
Then the world came looking. In Japan, there was a great appetite for uni, the golden roe inside the urchin. It sold fresh, at premium prices, at the fish auctions of Tokyo. Japan's own waters could not supply the demand. Buyers had already turned to California and British Columbia. As those beds were fished down, they turned to Maine.

VISUAL: Map pulls back to the Pacific: arcs from Japan to California and British Columbia, then a new arc to
Maine. On screen: "Uni: sea urchin roe".

SOURCE: Q17, T "1970s" (Maine DMR).

### Scene 1.3
NARRATION:
The boom was fast. In 1987, Maine landed 1.4 million pounds of urchins. In 1990, the catch was worth almost fifteen million dollars. By 1992, the landings passed twenty six million pounds. That year, the state began requiring a special urchin license, and issued eight hundred and seven hand-fishing licenses. A year later there were fourteen hundred and thirty nine. Divers and small boats crowded the coast. Among them were Dana and Denise Leavitt.

VISUAL: Animated bar chart, plain and large:
"1987: 1.4 million lb" · "1992: over 26 million lb" · "1990 value: almost $15 million".
Second card: "Hand-fishing urchin licenses: 807 (1992) · 1,439 (1993)".
Source line on screen: "Maine Department of Marine Resources; Christian Science Monitor, 1994".
End on `archival_dana-leavitt-drysuit-on-boat_SCAN0024.jpg`.

SOURCE: V10, T "1987", T "1990", T "1992".

## Chapter 2. The docks and Japan (2:30 to 4:45)

### Scene 2.1
NARRATION:
Dana and Denise were husband and wife, and they were commercial divers. They dove for sea urchin and scallops. And they sold the catch themselves, right on the Portland docks. Years later, the family described that start in a few words: early mornings, cold waters, and an uncompromising respect for the ocean. They called the business Great Atlantic Seafood.

VISUAL: `archival_dana-leavitt-drysuit-on-boat_SCAN0024.jpg`, slow push in. Lower third: "Dana Leavitt
(photo: Caviar Star)". Text card in typewriter style: "Great Atlantic Seafood · Portland, Maine".
No dock photos exist; use a drawn harbor map of Portland, not stock footage.

SOURCE: V1, V2, Q3, Q4, S1.

### Scene 2.2
NARRATION:
Urchin diving is cold, careful work. The best urchins live in shallow water, just under the kelp. Their spines are delicate. A broken spine lets salt water in and spoils the roe. And the best season in Maine runs from November to March. Winter diving.

VISUAL: Illustrated cross-section: kelp line, urchins on rock, diver silhouette, mesh bag. On screen:
"Prime season: November to March".

SOURCE: Maine DMR summary (maine-dmr_green-sea-urchin-fishery-summary.txt), background to V10.

### Scene 2.3
NARRATION:
At first, most Maine urchins were flown to Japan alive, whole, in their shells. Around 1992, plants in Portland began cracking them open and flying out the roe instead. Either way, for the Japanese market, quality was everything. Freshness. Color. Weight. How the roe was prepared. Pale yellow earned the highest grade. Maine's own fishery scientists wrote that the market did not get off the ground until Japanese representatives came to Maine to explain what their market required.

VISUAL: Graphic color card from pale yellow to orange to gray, top grade highlighted. Quote card:
"The Maine market did not get off the ground until Japanese representatives came to Maine to explain what is
required to satisfy their market." Maine Department of Marine Resources.

SOURCE: Q16, V11, T "1992" (CSM: processing began about 1992; DMR: whole urchins air-freighted vs roe extracted at the plant).

### Scene 2.4
NARRATION:
The Leavitts learned from them. A distributor's profile of the company puts it simply: they were trained by their Japanese counterparts, with strict quality control at every step. Their small local operation grew into a global business. Most of what they sold went to Japan.

VISUAL: `archival_japan-group-photo_dana-denise_SCAN0069.jpg`. Lower third: "Dana and Denise Leavitt in Japan
(photo: Caviar Star)". Then a slow cross-fade to `archival_japan-dinner-toast_dana-denise_gat-about.jpg`

SOURCE: Q8 (S2), V4, Q3.

### Scene 2.5
NARRATION:
Only a few photographs survive from those years, and most carry no date. A dinner, glasses raised. A busy street in Japan, with a camera date stamp from the nineteen nineties. What they show is a working relationship across the Pacific. Japan taught a standard. And that care for quality, the company says, carried into everything it would later do with caviar.

## Chapter 3. Building Great Atlantic (4:45 to 6:10)

### Scene 3.1
NARRATION:
Urchin was the beginning. Caviar became the passion. Alongside the uni, the Leavitts began to work with American wild caviar, and in time they sent caviar to Japan as well. In 1991, Great Atlantic Trading was established. That year is still on the company's logo.

VISUAL: Archival title card: "1991". The trident logo `gat_logo_estd-1991.png` draws on, "ESTD 1991" highlighted.

SOURCE: Q2, Q8, V3, V4.

### Scene 3.2
NARRATION:
American wild caviar comes from the great rivers in the middle of the country. Hackleback, a small sturgeon of the Missouri and the Mississippi. Paddlefish, the spoonbill. Bowfin, known in Louisiana as choupique. These are the roes Great Atlantic still lists among its American exports today. The company says it has delivered thousands of shipments to customers overseas.

VISUAL: Map of the Mississippi basin with the Missouri and White rivers traced. Species drawings appear one by
one: `gat_fish-drawing_hackleback.jpg`, `gat_fish-drawing_paddlefish.jpg`, `gat_fish-drawing_bowfin-black.jpg`,

SOURCE: V9, P (hackleback, paddlefish, bowfin rows), S12.

### Scene 3.3
NARRATION:
The urchin boom did not last forever. By the mid nineteen nineties, people in the industry were already worried that the urchin beds were being fished down. For the Leavitts, caviar and fine roe were taking center stage.

## Chapter 4. Caviar and the world after wild Caspian caviar (6:10 to 9:20)

### Scene 4.1
NARRATION:
To understand what Great Atlantic became, you have to understand what happened to caviar itself. For generations, the finest caviar came from wild sturgeon in the Caspian and Black Seas. Beluga. Osetra. Sevruga. Sturgeon are ancient fish. They can live up to a hundred years, and they do not spawn every year. They were fished close to the edge.

VISUAL: Map of the Caspian Sea, archival style. `baldor_cs_sturgeon-swimming.jpg` (flag: provenance of the
underlying image unknown, confirm rights) or a 3D sturgeon model. On screen: "Sturgeon can live up to 100 years".

SOURCE: V14 (IUCN: up to 100 years, do not reproduce annually), wikipedia_caviar_excerpts.txt.

### Scene 4.2
NARRATION:
The rules tightened, step by step. On April first, 1998, every species of sturgeon and paddlefish in the world came under CITES, the treaty on trade in endangered species. From then on, caviar could not cross a border without a permit. In 2004, the United States listed the beluga sturgeon as threatened. And on September thirtieth, 2005, it stopped imports of beluga caviar from the Caspian Sea.

VISUAL: Timeline cards, one per line, typed on:
"April 1, 1998: all sturgeon and paddlefish listed under CITES"
"October 21, 2004: beluga sturgeon listed as threatened (U.S.)"
"September 30, 2005: U.S. suspends Caspian beluga caviar imports"
Source line: "U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service".

SOURCE: V12, V13, T "Oct 21, 2004".

### Scene 4.3
NARRATION:
In 2010, the International Union for Conservation of Nature found that eighty five percent of sturgeon species were at risk of extinction. That made them the most threatened group of animals on its Red List.

VISUAL: Full-screen number: "85%". Under it: "of sturgeon species at risk of extinction · IUCN, 2010".

SOURCE: V14, Q18.

### Scene 4.4
NARRATION:
So the world's caviar had to come from somewhere else. It came from farms. Today, China produces more caviar than any other country. Farms in Italy, France, Poland, Israel and California raise sturgeon for many years before a single tin is filled.

VISUAL: World map, farm pins appear: China, Italy, France, Poland, Israel, California. Then
`cs_polish-caviar-farm.png` and `catalog2026_page05.jpg` (partner farms page). On screen: "China: the largest
caviar producer".

SOURCE: V15, wikipedia_caviar_excerpts.txt, catalog p.3 (Prunier 12 to 20 years).

### Scene 4.5
NARRATION:
In this world, an importer has to know exactly what is in every tin. The species. The origin. The permit. Great Atlantic's export page spells it out: every sturgeon and paddlefish lot carries its CITES number on the label, and a copy of the permit stays on file. The company says it is inspected every year, and works under the rules of the Fish and Wildlife Service, NOAA, the Department of Agriculture and the FDA.

VISUAL: 3D render of American Hackleback Sturgeon Caviar (`american-hackleback-sturgeon-caviar`) 1 oz jar
rotating; a label graphic with a placeholder "CITES lot no." field (generic, no real permit numbers).
Agency names as plain text on screen, no agency logos.

SOURCE: gat_american-export.txt, cs_sustainability.txt (company-stated), V12.

### Scene 4.6
NARRATION:
Wild American caviar still exists, in small amounts. Hackleback is, in the company's words, the only wild sturgeon species still commercially harvested in the United States. It depends on rivers and weather. In the winter of 2022, low water on the Mississippi kept the fish out of reach. The company's bowfin fishermen in Louisiana caught less than ten percent of their usual quota. Its partners said they had not seen conditions this poor in almost two decades.

VISUAL: Map of the lower Mississippi with water level graphic dropping. On screen: "2022/23 season:
bowfin catch under 10% of usual quota (Caviar Star's fishing partners)". Then `cs_product_bowfin-styled.jpg`.

SOURCE: P (hackleback claim, company-stated), S11, Q12, T "Jan 2, 2023".

### Scene 4.7
NARRATION:
Politics reaches the tin too. When war came to Ukraine in 2022, customers asked the family whether any of their caviar came from Russia. The answer they published was plain: no. In the same note, they said they were donating caviar to chefs' events raising money for Ukrainian relief and World Central Kitchen.

VISUAL: Simple text card on dark background: "March 28, 2022 · 'Do we still get caviar from Russia?'" then
"No." Then the 3D render of Lyna Polska Royal Osetra (`lyna-polska-royal-osetra-caviar`) with an on-screen
note "Osetra: the 'Russian sturgeon', farmed in Poland".

SOURCE: T "March 28, 2022" (cs_blog_blogdo-we-still-get-caviar-from-russia-russian-caviar-in-america.txt), P (Polska row).

## Chapter 5. Caviar Star (9:20 to 11:35)

### Scene 5.1
NARRATION:
Most of Great Atlantic's business is with other businesses. Distributors. Wholesalers. Chefs and caterers. But it also built a name of its own for the public. That name is Caviar Star.

### Scene 5.2
NARRATION:
No one has published the date Caviar Star began. But by December 2011, a food writer in New York was building a Christmas Eve dinner around five of its caviars, among them osetra, paddlefish and American white sturgeon. Today Caviar Star sells online across the country, packed to order and shipped overnight from Ocean Isle Beach.

### Scene 5.3
NARRATION:
The idea behind the brand is written on its own pages. "Caviar is a luxury, but at Caviar Star, it's a luxury meant to be enjoyed." True luxury, the family writes, should be inviting, not exclusive. They buy directly from farms and fisheries, without middlemen, and import in volume to keep prices within reach.

VISUAL: Quote card in serif type over `baldor_cs_circle-of-spoons.jpg`:
"Caviar is a luxury, but at Caviar Star, it's a luxury meant to be enjoyed."

### Scene 5.4
NARRATION:
Inside, the work is done by hand. Every container that arrives is inspected. The caviar is re-graded. Then it is hand-packed to order, every day, from large original tins into jars and tins from one ounce up to a kilo. The company describes more than eight thousand five hundred square feet of production, office and warehouse space, with three walk-in coolers and two freezers.

NARRATION:
In 2021, the magazine The Local Palate named Caviar Star among the South's best caviar. It reported that the family's small team moves nearly ten tons of caviar a year.
