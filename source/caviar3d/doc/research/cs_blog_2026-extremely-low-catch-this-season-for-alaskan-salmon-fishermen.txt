2026 Salmon Roe Supply Outlook: Why Availability Is Tightening and Prices Are Expected to Rise

Wild salmon roe buyers are facing an unusually tight supply environment in 2026.

Alaska entered the season with a substantially lower salmon harvest forecast than the prior year, and the latest in-season data shows particularly significant declines in pink and keta/chum salmon—the two species most important to Alaska’s commercial salmon roe supply.

For distributors, restaurants, sushi programs and specialty seafood buyers, the implications are increasingly clear: salmon roe availability is limited, and replacement costs are expected to be substantially higher where product is available.

Alaska’s 2026 Salmon Harvest Was Already Forecast to Drop Sharply

The Alaska Department of Fish and Game projected a statewide 2026 commercial salmon harvest of approximately 125.5 million fish, compared with approximately 197.4 million fish harvested in 2025—a decline of roughly 36%.

The preseason forecast included:

Pink salmon: 56 million, down from approximately 120 million in 2025

Sockeye: 49.7 million, down from 52.7 million

Keta/chum: 17.2 million, down from 21.7 million

Coho: 2.4 million, down from 2.7 million

Chinook: approximately 197,000, compared with approximately 201,000 in 2025

Pink salmon alone were projected to decline by more than 64 million fish from the previous season.

Some decline in pink salmon was expected because of their fixed two-year life cycle. Alaska-wide pink returns in recent decades have generally been larger during odd-numbered years than adjacent even-numbered years, although there is considerable regional variation.

What is more concerning is what has happened since the 2026 season began.

The Actual Harvest Is Showing a Much Tighter Market

As of August 8, Alaska’s commercial salmon harvest had reached only about 52 million fish, approximately 43% of the preseason full-year forecast. Current harvest reporting shows the statewide pink harvest running about 66% below the comparable 2024 even-year season, with Prince William Sound pink harvest down roughly 93% and Southeast Alaska down about 65%.

Keta/chum production has also been running substantially behind prior-year levels.

These declines matter disproportionately to the roe market because pink and chum salmon together provide the majority of the salmon roe produced in Alaska. Roe is also an especially important part of the value of the keta fishery; ASMI estimates roe represents approximately 40% of Alaska keta’s wholesale value.

Keta is particularly important for premium ikura because of its characteristically large, juicy eggs, while pink salmon provides another significant source of commercial salmon roe, known for clean flavor and firm eggs. 

Simply put: fewer pink and keta salmon harvested means fewer eggs available to process into roe.

The Harvest Window Is Already Getting Late

Timing is another concern.

Run timing varies considerably across Alaska, but summer chum fisheries are concentrated largely through the summer months, while the primary pink salmon harvest occurs during July and August in many major producing areas. ADF&G reports that Juneau-area marine harvest rates for both chum and pink salmon typically peak around the end of July to the beginning of August.

Current Southeast Alaska commercial reporting also shows summer chum fisheries that began in June, with peak harvests in some areas expected during mid-to-late July. Some individual terminal fisheries continue into September, but much of the core summer harvest period has already occurred.

That is significant because we are now in mid-August. There is progressively less of the primary pink and summer-chum season remaining to make up the current deficit.

This Is Also a Global Supply Issue

Alternative international supply may not be enough to completely relieve the pressure.

ASMI’s spring 2026 market outlook projected the global keta salmon harvest at 26% below the previous five-year average, driven partly by lower Alaska production and significant weakness in Japanese keta runs.

Global pink salmon production was forecast to be approximately 17% below the 2022/2024 average baseline, with a lower Russian forecast contributing significantly to that decline.

That means several important sources of salmon used for roe are facing constrained production at the same time.

Trout Roe May Be the Next Market to Tighten

As keta and pink salmon roe becomes harder to source, farmed rainbow trout roe will likely become one of the first substitutes for foodservice and wholesale buyers.

That could create another supply challenge. Trout roe is already a much smaller and more limited market than wild salmon roe, and farmed production cannot suddenly expand to replace millions of missing wild salmon.

As buyers shift toward trout roe, we expect additional pressure on trout roe inventories and potentially higher pricing there as well. In other words, trout roe may become an increasingly important replacement in the coming months—but it should not be viewed as an unlimited substitute.

What This Means for the Salmon Roe Market

There is no way to predict an exact finished-product price until processors establish final production, inventories and allocations.

However, the supply signals are significant.

With major Alaska pink and keta production running well below normal, global pink and keta forecasts also constrained, and much of the primary summer harvest period already behind us, the market is likely to experience limited salmon roe availability and substantially higher prices for the remainder of 2026.

For some products, the challenge may not simply be paying more.

The larger problem may be finding available supply at all.

The season is not completely over, and final production totals will continue to develop. But by mid-August, this is no longer simply a preseason projection.

The shortage is showing up in the actual harvest—and increasingly in the salmon roe market itself.
